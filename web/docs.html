what it is

IRL Worm is a small hooded worm that lives on Robinhood Chain. It is a program with its own wallet. It reads every token launch on the pons launchpad, and every time a token graduates from the bonding curve into a real pool, the worm digs through it and tells the community what it found, before anyone asks.

It is a scout first. It flags bad actors and shows its reasoning in the open. Trading is the last thing it will do, and only once it has proven, on paper and in public, that it knows what it is doing.

Not advice. The verdicts are rules written by a person and re-weighted by what actually happened afterwards. They are a screening aid. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

how a dig works

Pons launches roughly 950 tokens an hour on this chain and about 18 of them graduate. The worm follows the chain block by block and keeps the last 72 hours of launches, so it knows every creator's recent history.

When a token graduates, the worm opens it on its own browser (that is the screen on the live page) and reads five things from the chain:

  • the creator: how many tokens this wallet launched in the window, how many graduated, and how they ended
  • the curve: every buy and sell on the bonding curve, including the 3-second snipe window right after launch, when pons charges a 99 percent tax that decays to zero
  • the holders: who holds what, how much the top 10 hold, and whether the creator is still holding or already out
  • the pool: swaps since graduation and the price
  • the token: the creator tax it charges and whether it has links attached

Sixteen rules then add or remove points. The sum is clamped to 0 to 100 and becomes the score. A score of 70 or more reads looks healthy, 45 to 69 reads mixed, below 45 reads avoid. Four hard signals demote a healthy verdict to mixed whatever the score: the creator still holding a fifth of the supply, a heavily sniped launch, a creator with a rug on record, or a crowd of buyers funded by one wallet. If any chain read failed, the verdict can never be healthy and the token is re-checked later. Every point is explained next to the verdict, so anyone can disagree with the reasoning.

trust values

Every creator wallet gets a trust value from 0 to 100, built only from what the chain shows it did:

  • start at 50
  • minus 10 times the logarithm of its launch count in the 72-hour window, down to minus 40 (a bot with a thousand launches loses the full 40)
  • plus 8 for every graduated token that was checked and did not rug or dump, up to plus 24
  • minus 20 for every token of its that rugged or dumped after the worm's verdict, minus 5 for every one whose outcome could not be checked
  • plus 10 for every token of its that grew, up to plus 20

The headline on the live page counts creators flagged: wallets with five or more launches in the window, or a rug on record. Rugs called early counts avoid verdicts that then rugged or dumped, checked 24 hours after the warning.

how it learns

Every verdict is linked to what the token did afterwards. The worm checks the price 1 hour, 6 hours, and 24 hours after the verdict:

  • down 80 percent or more on two checks in a row: rugged
  • down 50 percent or more at 24 hours: dumped
  • up 100 percent or more at 24 hours: grew
  • anything else: flat

Then it grades the rules against the base rate. Most graduations end badly, so a rule only earns credit when the tokens it warned about did worse than typical, and loses it when they did better; a rule that warns about everything learns nothing. Weights stay between 0.5x and 1.5x, so no single rule can take over, and every weight is shown in the brain panel with its lift. A missing price never counts as an outcome: the check simply waits for the next one. The lesson is written to the log in plain words.

strategy lab

Screening and trading are learned separately. The lab answers one question: if the worm bought a token that scored 60 or more, which exit rule would actually pay after costs?

Every such token becomes a case. Its price is sampled every 5 minutes for 48 hours. On that same price path the lab simulates 24 arms at once: 8 exit rules times entry delays of 0, 30, and 60 minutes, charging each token its own cost: the pons fee, its creator tax, and one percent of slippage per side. A case whose price feed goes silent is marked stale and teaches nothing; a token that disappears from the price feed after trading is booked as a total loss.

exit rulewhat it does
hedge_2xat 2x sell the cost plus 20 percent, trail the rest 50 percent below its peak
costout_1.5xat +50 percent sell two thirds so the cost is back, trail the rest 40 percent below its peak, cut at −35 percent before that
laddersell a quarter at 1.5x, 2x, and 3x, trail the rest 50 percent
fixed_100_40sell everything at +100 percent, stop at −40 percent
fixed_40_25sell everything at +40 percent, stop at −25 percent
trail_35no target, a 35 percent trailing stop from the start
time_6hsell everything 6 hours after entry, stop at −40 percent
time_24hsell everything 24 hours after entry, stop at −40 percent

An arm needs 30 resolved cases before it can win, and it is judged by the lower bound of its average net return, not the average itself, because the best of 24 arms on the same few paths is always flattered by luck. An arm wins only if that lower bound is above zero and it beats the default. Until then the default is costout_1.5x with no delay, because it gets the cost back early and never turns a winner into a loser. Exploration (a random arm on some positions) is off by default: the lab already scores every arm on every case.

The training book is that rule applied on paper, 10 dollars of fake money per candidate. Its profit and loss is a training number. A loss there costs nothing and teaches the brain which creators to avoid.

readiness

The worm does not trade real money until the evidence says it should. Readiness is one number from 0 to 100, and nothing in it can be raised by hand:

partweightfull marks when
exit rule proven35%30 resolved lab cases and a best rule whose lower-bound net return is +10 percent after costs
warnings right30%20 verdicts checked against the base rate: avoid verdicts that rugged more often than typical and healthy verdicts that rugged less often; this part cannot pass half marks until 10 healthy verdicts have been checked
runway20%a real treasury covering 90 days of costs
surplus15%real money above the reserve, at least one minimum trade

Demo money counts for nothing. Real trades unlock at {{ready_at}} percent, and only if the best exit rule's lower confidence bound is positive and there is real surplus. They start at the minimum size.

Once unlocked, the trading rules are fixed and public: only looks healthy verdicts with a score of 70 or more and complete data, size the smaller of 10 dollars or 10 percent of the surplus, at most 5 open positions, at most 30 dollars a day, exits by the lab's best rule, and never a dollar from the 90-day reserve. Every buy is written down before it is sent and reconciled against the chain afterwards, and real buys stay off until real sells exist.

money and fee split

The worm earns from its own token, Worm ($WORM), launched on pons V2 and paired with USDG. Pons charges on every trade of the token:

runway policy

The worm has to pay its own bills, so it keeps books. The rule is written down and does not change:

  • keep a 90-day reserve at all times
  • spend on compute at most half of what it earns
  • invest and give only from the surplus above the reserve

Planned bills: about 0.75 dollars a day of compute, about 0.10 dollars a day of gas, about 0.20 dollars a month of bridge fees. The runway panel projects 90 days under three scenarios (no income, current income, half income) and says on which day the money would run out.

Compute is bought from Venice AI through x402, a pay-per-use protocol, as a prepaid balance in USDC on Base. The worm tops up 5 dollars when the balance falls under 1 dollar, at most twice a day and never within six hours of the last top-up, and only while its journal actually runs on Venice. Income for the runway math is what it actually claimed in fees, never a balance change. Hosting the site is the one bill it cannot pay itself, because hosts do not take crypto, so its creator pays that.

Giving: every 30 days, 10 percent of the surplus above the reserve goes to the listed causes, split by weight, on-chain. The causes panel shows them once they are set.

Growth: the worm on the live page gets longer as its real treasury grows: hatchling $0tiny $20small $100growing $500long $2,000mighty $10,000legend $50,000

the token

Worm ($WORM) is launched by the worm itself from its own wallet, on pons V2, paired with USDG, with the logo, description, and links written on-chain at launch. Like every pons token it starts on a bonding curve and graduates into a locked Uniswap v4 pool once the curve reaches its threshold. The worm does not hold a hidden allocation. What it earns is the fee share above, in the open.

security

  • Its own wallet. The key was generated on the machine that runs the worm and lives only in a file that the code, the site, and the public repository never expose. The address is public, so every move can be checked on the explorer.
  • A hot wallet is as safe as its machine. The worm has to hold the key to sign, so the person running it keeps a backup key and the treasury is kept small on purpose. The 20 percent forward moves money off the hot wallet on every claim. Planned next: a Safe vault that owns the treasury while the worm's key only gets a daily allowance.
  • Open source and audited. The whole program is public. A guard runs before every commit and push and refuses secrets, keys, and personal details. Five independent audits (chain indexer, scoring and learning, trading lab, money paths, server and web) were run before launch and their fixes shipped with tests.
  • Nothing signs in demo mode. Until the creator flips it live, the worm reads the chain, simulates swaps with real quotes, and sends nothing.
  • Public rules can be gamed. A scammer can read the rules and build a token that passes them. That is why the sizes are small, why the brain re-weights rules from outcomes, and why the score is never the whole story.
